## STORY 1: Opportunity — "Built for 90 days"
Launch/landing times: NASA's NSSDCA lists launch as July 8, 2003 and landing as January 25, 2004 in UTC; the same moments were July 7 and January 24 in US time. Pick one convention and say which.
Planet: Mars | Planned: 90 sols | Actual: contact until sol 5,111 (about 14 years)

1. **Built for 90 days.** Opportunity launched on July 7, 2003 and landed on Mars on January 24, 2004 (US time), on a flat plain called Meridiani Planum. Scientists picked it because orbiters had spotted a mineral there, hematite, that usually forms in water. NASA planned for it to work for 90 Martian days, called sols. Its twin, Spirit, had landed three weeks earlier.
2. **Following the water.** Opportunity found tiny gray spheres nicknamed "blueberries" and layered rocks. These clues told scientists that liquid water once soaked this part of Mars. NASA says the twin rovers confirmed that water was long-standing on Mars in ancient times. [VERIFY the 'blueberries' detail]
3. **It kept going.** Ninety sols passed. Then years. Opportunity drove across plains and explored huge craters, travelling 45.16 kilometers (28.06 miles) in total, a record for any rover that stood for years. Sometimes wind blew dust off its solar panels and gave it fresh power.
4. **The storm.** Opportunity had survived a big dust storm in 2007, so the team hoped it would again. [VERIFY on NASA 2007 press release] In June 2018 a giant dust storm grew over Mars. In February Opportunity made 628 watt-hours of energy in a day. By June 10 it made just 22. The dust was blocking almost all the sunlight. (Use the chart here.)
5. **Silence.** Opportunity's last message came on June 10, 2018, sol 5,111. The team kept sending recovery signals for months, more than 1,000 of them. [VERIFY on NASA page] On February 13, 2019, NASA declared the mission complete. [VERIFY]

**Bonus fact (NASA-verified):** Opportunity held the off-Earth driving record until October 11, 2026, when Perseverance passed it. Use it as a closing hook: "The baton has been passed."

**Closing line (label as illustrative, not a real NASA quote):**
"I was built for 90 sols. I explored for thousands. My mission ended, but what I found is still teaching you."

**Why it matters:** Solar power is great, but dust can starve it. Engineers think about this when they design power systems for future Mars missions.

**Then vs Now:** planned 90 sols -> actual 5,111 sols of contact.

## STORY 2: Apollo 11 Eagle — "The machine that stayed behind"
Planet: Moon | Landed: July 20, 1969 | Left behind: the descent stage and more

1. **A ride to the Moon.** Apollo 11 launched on July 16, 1969. Astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin flew a lander named Eagle down to the Sea of Tranquility. Michael Collins stayed in orbit in the command module, Columbia. The landing was on July 20.
2. **Two machines in one.** Eagle had two parts. The bottom part, the descent stage, had the landing engine, legs and storage. The top part, the ascent stage, held the crew cabin and the engine for going home.
3. **Leaving it behind.** To lift off, the astronauts used the descent stage as a launch pad. The ascent stage flew back up to meet Columbia. The descent stage stayed on the Moon, along with a plaque and science equipment. [VERIFY plaque wording if you quote it]
4. **Still useful.** The astronauts placed a laser reflector on the surface. Scientists still bounce laser beams off reflectors left by Apollo missions to measure the distance to the Moon. [VERIFY that Apollo 11's own reflector is still in use]
5. **Seen from space.** NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed the landing site. The pictures show the descent stage and the trails the astronauts walked. [VERIFY image ID and credit on NASA LROC page]

**Closing line (illustrative):** "I helped you land. I stayed so you could go home. I'm still here, quietly waiting to be seen."

**Why it matters:** Parts of a mission are designed to be left behind. Future crews will plan landers, and even their leftovers, with the Moon's history in mind.

## STORY 3: Sojourner — "The little rover that proved it could"
Planet: Mars | Landed: July 4, 1997 [VERIFY] | Planned: 7 sols | Actual: about 83 sols [VERIFY]

1. **Tiny and brave.** Sojourner was about the size of a microwave oven and weighed roughly 10.5 kilograms (23 pounds). It rode to Mars on the Pathfinder lander, which landed on July 4, 1997. [VERIFY]
2. **First wheels on Mars.** Sojourner was the first rover to drive on Mars. It rolled off the lander and inspected nearby rocks, including ones nicknamed Barnacle Bill and Yogi. [VERIFY names]
3. **A science lab on wheels.** It carried an instrument called APXS that measured which elements are in rocks and soil. The team could test rocks without sending any people.
4. **Beyond the plan.** NASA planned for about 7 sols. Sojourner and Pathfinder kept working for about 83 sols. [VERIFY] Their last signal arrived on September 27, 1997. [VERIFY]
5. **Quiet at last.** Engineers think the lander's battery may have failed. [VERIFY wording] Sojourner never got to drive again, but it proved that small rovers work on Mars.

**Closing line (illustrative):** "I was small. I was early. Every rover after me drove on the idea that I proved."

**Why it matters:** Sojourner showed that a rover could survive and do science on Mars. That helped lead to Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity and Perseverance.

**Then vs Now:** planned 7 sols -> about 83 sols.
